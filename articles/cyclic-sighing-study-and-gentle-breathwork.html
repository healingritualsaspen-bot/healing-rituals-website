What the cyclic-sighing study found—and why gentler is still a valid choice

A clear reading of a prominent breathing study, including its short duration, remote design and limits for individual practice.

A promising study is not a universal prescription

Cyclic sighing has received attention because it was compared directly with other brief breathing practices and mindfulness meditation. The result is interesting, but a named protocol should not become a demand to breathe through discomfort.

What participants actually did

Balban and colleagues randomized adults to three structured breathing practices or mindfulness meditation for five minutes a day over 28 days. All groups improved in positive affect; the exhale-emphasized cyclic-sighing group showed the largest daily increase in positive affect and a reduction in respiratory rate. Read the study.

Why the comparison needs context

This was a remotely conducted short study in volunteers, not a clinical trial of Healing Rituals’ breathwork or a treatment comparison for diagnosed anxiety. Participants knew which practice they received, and the findings do not show that more intense breathing is better.

Adaptation is part of safety

If repeated sighs create dizziness, tingling, panic, pressure or air hunger, stop and return to ordinary breathing. People with relevant respiratory, cardiovascular, pregnancy, fainting or panic concerns should seek individualized advice. Continue with comfortable pacing, comfort over intensity, or gentle breathwork.

The smallest effective version may be the best one

Research protocols are designed for comparison, not to establish a universal daily requirement. In personal practice, one or two comfortable, unforced sighs may be plenty—or ordinary breathing may be the better choice. Do not combine techniques, extend breath holds or increase intensity in pursuit of a stronger effect. A sustainable practice should leave you oriented and able to continue your day, not depleted, euphoric or frightened.
