When one person pursues and the other retreats: interrupting the demand–withdraw loop

Recognize the demand–withdraw relationship pattern and prepare a smaller, safer conversation without blaming either role.

From Healing Rituals · Published September 25, 2026

In this article

The loop is larger than either role

One partner asks, presses or criticizes; the other becomes quiet, changes the subject or leaves. Each response can intensify the other. The pursuing partner experiences distance as proof that more pressure is needed, while the withdrawing partner experiences pressure as proof that escape is necessary.

What relationship research observed

Demand–withdraw patterns have been observed in relationship research and linked with distress, though early studies often framed the pattern through gender and social roles that do not describe every couple. Read the foundational study record. Observation does not identify a villain, and it is not evidence for a wellness session as couples therapy.

Change the shape of the conversation

Name the pattern without diagnosing the person: “We are in the push-and-pull again.” Agree on one question, a short time window and a specific return time if either person needs a pause. The person who tends to pursue practices a clear request; the person who tends to withdraw practices a clear return.

Safety comes before mutual process

This shared-process framing is inappropriate where there is coercion, intimidation, violence or fear of retaliation. Seek specialized individual support and safety planning. Couples wellness does not replace licensed couples therapy. Continue with regulation before repair, preparing to talk, or relationship support.
