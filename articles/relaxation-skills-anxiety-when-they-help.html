Relaxation skills and anxiety: support, not a demand to calm down

Relaxation training can help anxiety, yet forcing calm or focusing inward too quickly can increase discomfort for some people.

From Healing Rituals · Published September 18, 2026

The goal is flexibility, not permanent calm

Anxiety is a protective system, not a personal failure. A relaxation practice may help lower arousal or widen attention, but it should not become proof that you are safe or a ritual you must perform perfectly. The broader aim is having more than one way to respond while continuing appropriate treatment and real-world problem solving.

What the review found

Manzoni and colleagues reviewed 27 studies of relaxation training for anxiety and found a medium overall effect, with larger effects among volunteers than clinical patients. Read the full review. Interventions and study quality varied. The findings support relaxation as a potentially useful component, not a universal treatment or evidence that every guided-rest, breathing or energy practice has the same effect.

When inward attention feels worse

Closing the eyes, slowing down or noticing the body can sometimes amplify panic sensations, intrusive thoughts or dissociation. Keep eyes open, orient to the room, shorten the practice or stop. Choose external anchors such as color, sound or contact with the floor. A practitioner should respond to discomfort with choice, not insist that distress means the method is working.

Match support to severity

For everyday anxiety, a brief predictable practice may be enough to create a pause. Persistent, worsening or function-limiting anxiety deserves licensed assessment. Mania, psychosis, suicidal thinking or inability to care for yourself requires urgent help. Read grounding when anxiety rises, breathing and meditation research, and the anxiety support overview.
