An energy diary without quotas: noticing patterns in chronic fatigue

Use an energy diary to notice delayed symptom changes while avoiding fixed increases, self-blame and pushing through post-exertional malaise.

Tracking should reduce uncertainty, not create a quota

With chronic fatigue, especially when post-exertional malaise is present, the effects of activity may appear hours or days later. A diary can help reveal patterns, but it should not become a scorecard or a demand to increase activity each week.

What energy management means

NICE guidance describes energy management as a flexible, person-led approach across physical, cognitive, emotional and social activity. It advises staying within current energy limits and explicitly advises against fixed incremental increases such as graded exercise therapy. Read the guidance.

A diary that includes delayed effects

Keep the record simple: activity, approximate effort, symptoms at the time, and symptoms later that day or over the next two days. Include cognitive and social exertion, not only steps. Look for ranges and delayed responses rather than a single “safe” number. If tracking itself consumes energy or increases anxiety, shorten it or stop.

Rest practices must respect the illness

Persistent or worsening fatigue needs medical assessment because many conditions can contribute. Rest, sound or gentle guided attention may support comfort, but they do not treat the underlying illness. Do not push through post-exertional malaise. Explore pacing, research limits, or chronic-fatigue support.
