What guided imagery research teaches us about a short period of rest

A short guided practice may shift an immediate state, but rest does not need to become another outcome to perfect.

From Healing Rituals · Published September 18, 2026

Rest can be useful without becoming sleep

Guided Deep Rest is an invitation to reduce effort while remaining free to drift, listen or stay awake. The most workable aim is not to manufacture a named brain state. It is to create conditions in which attention has fewer demands. If sleep comes, that is fine; if it does not, the period can still be restorative in an ordinary, experiential sense.

A small randomized comparison

In a study of 60 healthy undergraduates, participants completed a single 20-minute recorded session of guided imagery, deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation or control. All three active exercises increased self-reported relaxation relative to control, while physiological responses were not identical. Read the full study. This was an immediate laboratory-style comparison, not evidence that twenty minutes replaces hours of sleep or treats insomnia.

Choose the least effortful doorway

Some people settle through imagery; others prefer body sensation, sound or simple orientation to the room. The right instruction is one you do not have to fight. Support your body, reduce avoidable interruptions and let the guidance be background rather than an assignment. If closing your eyes increases unease, keep them softly open. If imagery becomes activating, return to present-time sights and sounds.

Let the next hour be part of the practice

Before beginning, decide what follows. A demanding call immediately after rest may erase the contrast. Leave a few minutes to sit up, drink water and notice what pace feels available. Explore daytime rest without chasing sleep, guided rest and sleep research, and the Guided Deep Rest overview.
