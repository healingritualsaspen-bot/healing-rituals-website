Retreat design

Nature, attention and spaciousness: designing a retreat without overfilling it

What greenspace research can suggest—and not prove—about leaving room for nature and recovery in a private retreat.

From Healing Rituals · Published September 25, 2026

In this article

Space is part of the design

A retreat can become crowded with worthy activities. When every hour is assigned, there may be little room to notice what has shifted. Time outdoors can offer a natural transition, but it does not need to be strenuous, scenic or optimized to count.

What greenspace research can tell us

A large systematic review and meta-analysis linked greenspace exposure with several health outcomes, while emphasizing substantial variation in study design, exposure definitions and risk of bias. Read the review. Much of the evidence was observational, so it cannot prove that nature caused the differences or that a private retreat produces them.

Build around transitions

Consider the rhythm around an experience: a quiet arrival, unhurried recovery and a short period without conversation or screens. Nature time might mean sitting near a window, a level walk or listening outdoors. Accessibility, altitude, weather and energy limits matter more than an idealized itinerary.

Aspen is a setting, not a prescription

Aspen and the Roaring Fork Valley can be beautiful containers for reflection, yet landscape is not a treatment. Visitors should plan for altitude, hydration, medications and mobility needs with appropriate professionals. Explore retreat planning, re-entry, or private retreats.
